You have heard the term in a thousand different contexts: companies, NGOs, social media, political speeches. Everyone uses it and few define it. That matters more than it sounds, because if you do not know exactly what it is, you cannot know whether you are creating it or only believing you are.

## The three criteria

Social impact is **sustained**, **measurable** and **traceable** change in the lives of people or communities, as the result of a specific intervention. If any of the three is missing, what you have is social activity. It is an uncomfortable distinction, and it is the only useful one.

**Sustained** means the change is still there when the intervention ends. Handing out food during an emergency relieves; teaching a family to grow their own food changes something that still works five years later. Immediate aid holds a situation up. Impact changes it.

**Measurable** means you can demonstrate it with data, not with impressions. How many people went from not reading to reading. How many families left extreme poverty. How many young people did not drop out. Without measurement you do not know whether your work is working or only consuming resources with good intentions.

**Traceable** means you can draw the line between what you did and what happened. Plenty of organizations claim results produced by factors they never controlled. Isolating your own effect from everything else is hard, which is why almost nobody does it.

## The four components

Impact happens when all four of these are present at the same time:

- **A real diagnosis** of the need, before intervening.
- **A design that fits** the specific context, not an off-the-shelf solution.
- **Sustained execution** for as long as the change actually needs.
- **Honest evaluation**, with a willingness to change what did not work.

The most common failure is skipping the first one. An organization arrives with the solution already decided, having asked nothing. It spends resources on something that does not address the real problem. The community is grateful for the attention, and its situation does not change.

## Social activity and social impact, side by side

A Christmas gift drive in a struggling neighbourhood produces joy, photographs, and the feeling of having done something good. That is social activity, and it has value.

A tutoring programme running for several years, tracking each child and measuring school outcomes, changes entire trajectories. That is social impact.

Both are worth doing. They are not the same thing and they do not produce the same result.

## The computer case

A company donated computers to public schools for five years. The machines sat in storage: there were no trained teachers and no connectivity to use them.

They changed approach. They stopped donating hardware and started funding teacher training and internet access. Students' digital learning outcomes went up sixfold, on half the budget.

The equipment was never the constraint. The capacity to use it was.

## The patience almost nobody has

Real change takes years. An organization that needs results this quarter will produce a great deal of activity and very little impact — and will be able to show numbers that look excellent.

As someone who wants to contribute, you have two legitimate paths. Serve from immediacy, responding to what is urgent. Or serve from transformation, committing to processes whose results you will see several years out. Both are needed. Knowing which one fits you is what lets you keep going.

## What the numbers cannot say

Some transformations are not quantifiable and are just as real: the trust built when someone comes back every week, the shift in how a family sees itself. Measuring matters. Believing that only the measurable exists does not.
