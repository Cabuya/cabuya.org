Reading about social impact in the abstract convinces very few people. Examples convince.

One warning first: what follows describes **shapes of intervention** that recur across the sector's literature and documented programmes, not cases we have audited. We invent no percentages and attribute no figures to specific projects. If an article gives you exact numbers without saying where they came from, be suspicious — including this one.

## Five shapes that work

### Education combined with productive training, in rural areas

Pairing school continuity with agricultural or technical training for young people in municipalities affected by conflict.

It works because it addresses both reasons a rural teenager drops out: school does not appear to lead anywhere, and income is needed now. The results only become visible ten years later, when those young people are still in their territory creating local employment.

### Community health built on local knowledge

Training leaders from the community itself as health promoters, connecting western medicine with the territory's traditional practices.

It works because the promoter does not leave. The outside organization eventually withdraws; she still lives there. It is the clearest example of the sustainability test: the change survives the exit.

### Reintegration with integrated support

Technical training, seed capital, psychosocial support and work with the receiving community, all at once.

Programmes that hand over capital alone produce relapse. Those that add psychosocial support and work with the community receiving people produce very different results. The variable is not the money.

### Housing paired with financial education

Participatory construction accompanied by training in managing resources.

Handing over the house alone is not enough: without the capacity to sustain it, the housing deteriorates or gets sold. The most interesting effect shows up in the next generation, in whether the children stay in school.

### Cooperatives of women producers

Production, processing and direct sale, organised collectively over years.

What changes is not only income: it is those women's position inside their own households and communities. The economic dimension pulls the political one along, and that is where change becomes hard to reverse.

## The four elements they share

1. **Long-term work** — five years minimum. None of these produces anything in one.
2. **The community as protagonist**, not as passive recipient of somebody else's design.
3. **Rigorous measurement from the start**, with a baseline. Without it, none of the above can be demonstrated.
4. **Integrated models** addressing several dimensions at once rather than one isolated variable.

## The five mistakes they avoided

- Arriving with the solution already decided.
- Chasing immediate results for a report.
- Depending on a single funder.
- Centring the implementing organization instead of the community.
- Abandoning when difficulties appeared, instead of adjusting the strategy and keeping the objective.

## The uncomfortable lesson

**Quality matters more than quantity.**

One well-designed intervention with a hundred people over five years produces more transformation than a thousand shallow interventions with thousands of people over one year.

It is a hard truth for organizations that need to show large numbers to their funders, and it is what the data shows over and over.

## How to recognise an organization like that

More than five years of operation. Documented methodology. External evaluations. And a willingness to show what did not work alongside what did.

An organization that only publishes wins and never lessons should raise questions: every real intervention has failures, and whoever does not report them is either not measuring them or not telling you.
