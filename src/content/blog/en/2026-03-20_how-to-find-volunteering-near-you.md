You want to help, but nearby. You have no way of crossing the city every week to reach a foundation on the far side of it.

That is where almost everyone gets stuck. You search for "volunteering near me" and get a mess of results, paid ads, distant foundations and information from five years ago.

## Three filters before you search

### 1. Your real radius

Five minutes? Fifteen? An hour?

Plenty of people take on distant projects out of enthusiasm and abandon them within weeks because the logistics wear them down. Start close; you can expand later if you want.

### 2. Your honest availability

Mornings, afternoons, evenings? Weekdays or weekends?

This filters as much as distance does, because organizations run on schedules set by the service: a community kitchen operates at midday, a night shelter at night, an education project around school hours.

### 3. The kind of activity

Some options mean direct contact with people; others are logistics or administration behind the scenes. Some are physical, some intellectual. Some are emotionally intense.

Knowing what you actually prefer keeps you from committing to something you will not sustain.

## Why the best ones do not surface on Google

Many small and mid-sized foundations run with no marketing budget, so they do not appear in the first results. They tend to be precisely where your contribution matters most.

Specialised platforms exist for that: giving visibility to organizations that would otherwise be invisible, with real schedules, a clear description of what is needed, and direct contact. That cuts the time between intention and first action from weeks to minutes.

## Two routes almost nobody uses

**Walk your own neighbourhood one afternoon, looking.** There are probably churches, community boards, schools, care homes and kitchens a few blocks away that you had never noticed.

**Talk to older neighbours.** Someone who has lived there for decades knows organizations that are not online and have been doing serious work for years. That word-of-mouth information is still the most reliable there is.

## Do not commit to the first one

Visit two or three. Talk to whoever coordinates volunteers. Watch the atmosphere, the organization, and above all how the people being served are treated.

Two weeks of looking around means that when you do commit, it is to a place you want to be every week for the next few years.

## The thirty-minute rule

Proximity is not about comfort. It is about staying.

If getting there takes more than thirty minutes, you will eventually stop going. If it takes ten, you hold the commitment for years without needing extraordinary motivation.

And since the volunteering that changes anything is not the one-off event but the constant presence, that logistical detail ends up being the substantive one.
