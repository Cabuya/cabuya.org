You want to be part of something that produces real impact, not well-presented social activity. The problem is that from the outside they are hard to tell apart — and organizations doing shallow work often look more professional than the ones changing something.

## The seven signals

### 1. Methodological clarity

A serious project explains how it works, what method it follows, and why it chose that approach rather than another.

If all you get is photographs and moving testimonials, there is no technical basis behind it. Emotion is easy to produce; method is not.

### 2. Rigorous measurement

They can show you before-and-after data, a baseline, concrete indicators.

If there are only loose anecdotes, it is probably not producing the sustained change it believes it is producing.

### 3. A sustained track record

Fifteen years with the same community means deep understanding. One year switching topics periodically means the surface.

Transformation needs time, and there is no shortcut.

### 4. The community in the lead

In a project with real impact, the people served take part in the design and the evaluation. Their perspective guides decisions.

Ask directly how interventions get designed and who decides. The answer tells you nearly everything.

### 5. Financial transparency

They publish their numbers, explain where the money comes from and where it goes.

Anyone who evades that question — or takes offence at it — usually has management problems that end up affecting the impact.

### 6. A network of partners

Serious projects work with other organizations, local government, universities, companies.

Working in isolation is not a sign of independence. It is usually a sign that nobody else wanted to work with them.

### 7. A professional team

People who have dedicated their careers to this, with training and experience, not only well-meaning volunteers.

Willingness alone does not produce sustained impact. Technical capability is required, and only time gives that.

## Once you are in: four attitudes

**Arrive with humility.** Learn before proposing. Social contexts have rules you only learn from the inside.

**Commit to the full cycle.** Coming and going costs more than it contributes.

**Contribute from your real strengths**, not from where you think you ought to contribute.

**Take feedback.** You will do things badly in the first month. Everyone does.

## The mistake that does the most damage

Arriving with your own agenda.

Plenty of people show up convinced they know what the community needs and want to impose their idea. That can destroy years of careful work, and whoever does it almost never finds out.

Effective participation requires putting the collective project above your own judgement, at least until you understand why things are done the way they are. Anyone who cannot do that does less damage contributing on their own.
