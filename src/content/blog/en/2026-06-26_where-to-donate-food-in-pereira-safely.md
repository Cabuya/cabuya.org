You have food to donate and you want to be sure it reaches whoever needs it. That concern is the right one: the difference between a useful donation and one that gets in the way is almost always in the operational details.

## What each kind of organization accepts

**Food banks.** They take volume and have processes to redistribute. They are the safest route if you have a large or varied quantity.

**Children's foundations.** They need specific products for supplementary feeding: fruit, fresh vegetables, dairy, protein, nutritionally balanced food for growing children. Not everything works; ask first, according to the age group they serve.

**Shelters for people living on the street.** They serve daily breakfast and lunch. The most useful items are non-perishables that sustain the operation without straining storage: grains, rice, cooking oil, tinned goods.

## The four protocols

1. **Check expiry dates.** Anything expired or near expiry creates a health problem, not a donation.
2. **Keep the cold chain** on refrigerated items throughout transport.
3. **Pack hygienically and label it.** Whoever receives it has to know what it is without opening it.
4. **Arrange the delivery.** Turning up unannounced can mean nobody is there, or nowhere to store it.

## Home-cooked food: almost always no

However good the intention, organizations have food safety protocols that prevent them accepting prepared food without handling guarantees.

It is not distrust of you. It is that food poisoning among children or older people has consequences no organization can absorb.

If you want to cook, ask whether you can do it **in their kitchen**, under their protocols. The answer is often yes.

## The most useful is usually raw

Rice, grains, cooking oil, flour, non-perishables generally.

They let the organization cook to its own protocols and plan balanced menus, and they store for weeks. A donation like that sustains the operation long past the day you delivered it.

## The alternative almost nobody considers

**Donating the equivalent in money** so the organization buys.

They get better wholesale prices, they know exactly what is missing this week, and you avoid the transport logistics.

If a kitchen serves two hundred lunches a day, it knows better than you what needs buying.

## Coordinate in a group

Food banks recommend this explicitly: when a building, a company or a group of friends organises a monthly collection, the volume is larger and the logistics get simpler for whoever receives it.

It is considerably more effective than many individual bags arriving at different destinations with no coordination.

## Call first

Needs change with the season, with campaigns under way, and with current inventory.

One call prevents you bringing something they cannot take, or something that goes to waste because that category is already covered.
