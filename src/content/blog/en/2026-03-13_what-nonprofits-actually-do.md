Nonprofits carry a public image that mixes admiration with suspicion. People accept that they do important work, and at the same time doubts circulate about where the money goes and what happens between one photograph and the next.

The real work is worth looking at. It is considerably less photogenic and considerably larger.

## Four dimensions at once

### Programme design

A diagnosis of the territory, a review of what has worked on similar problems, specific and measurable objectives, activities with a defined frequency and duration, the resources required, indicators for tracking progress.

This technical work is what separates a serious organization from a well-meaning improvisation. It is also the part nobody sees.

### Implementation

Constant coordination of professional and volunteer teams, logistics for materials, working with local authorities and other partners, handling whatever goes wrong, responding to critical situations — and keeping a team motivated through long stretches in sometimes difficult conditions.

### Accompaniment

Individual or family follow-up, spotting needs that surface mid-programme, connecting with other organizations when complementary services are required, and building the trust without which no process of change holds.

### Evaluation

Systematically collecting information during and after, analysing it to find out what worked and what did not, adjusting methods on the evidence, and accounting to funders, beneficiaries and the public.

## One full day

At a foundation working with children:

**Morning.** Team meeting to review progress. The educators plan the day's activities. The social worker goes over cases needing individual attention. The coordinator takes a call with a funder. The administrator processes supplier payments.

**Afternoon.** Programmes run with the groups of children. The day gets documented. Meetings with families. Home visits where the case calls for one.

**End of day.** Planning tomorrow.

That rhythm holds for years, on permanently tight budgets, with salaries below the private sector, and a constant tension between what the population needs and what is available.

## The part that never reaches the feed

They also do advocacy: identifying structural problems, proposing changes to public policy, supplying technical information to decision-makers, moving public opinion.

That work produces no photographs and usually creates more change per peso than direct operation, because it acts on the cause rather than the symptom.

## The mistake when judging them

Judging them by their communications instead of their impact.

The most serious organizations often communicate worse: they have no content team and would rather spend on operations. The ones communicating most sophisticatedly sometimes invest disproportionately in image.

It is not a rule, but it is worth holding in mind before deciding who to support.

## The structural difference

Every peso a nonprofit receives goes back into its mission. There are no shareholders waiting for dividends and no owners looking for a return.

That does not automatically make them good, or efficient. It makes them evaluable on something else: not on profitability, but on whether the change they promised actually happened.
